# Thought Experiment

Work out what an idea implies by constructing cases in which it must say something definite.

## Operation

1. State the claim, mechanism, or distinction under examination and the premises it rests on. Keep the user's version; do not substitute a weaker one.
2. Construct a small number of cases, each designed to isolate one question. Useful moves:
   - **Vary one factor:** hold everything else fixed and change the factor the claim says matters.
   - **Remove the proposed cause:** a case with the outcome but not the cause, or the cause but not the outcome.
   - **Push to an extreme:** take a variable to its limit and see whether the claim still holds.
   - **Transplant:** move the mechanism to a setting where its assumptions differ.
   - **Separate entangled factors:** a case where two things the claim treats as one come apart.
3. For each case, state what the claim predicts and, where they differ, what rival views predict.
4. Report what the cases expose: unintended implications, counterexamples, hidden variables, boundary conditions, or distinctions the claim needs.
5. Identify which cases could be checked against real-world evidence, and how.

Keep the cases concrete and few. A case earns its place by showing something the others do not.

## Delegation

Construct cases directly. When the conversation strongly favors one view and the host supports isolated workers, a worker may build cases from the stated premises without the favored conclusion. The conversational agent checks that the cases test the actual premises and owns the result.

## Boundaries

- Do not report a thought experiment's result as evidence about the world. It shows what follows if the premises hold, not that they hold.
- Do not build cases on impossible or irrelevant conditions unless the claim itself is meant to cover them.
- Do not turn the exercise into a list of objections; that is CHALLENGE.
- Do not resolve the empirical question a case raises; say how it could be checked. That is VERIFY or RESEARCH.
