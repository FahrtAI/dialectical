# Collection gaps

Find the missing evidence worth seeking, not every fact that could be collected.

## Operation

- Infer the relevant judgment, question, or competing explanations from context. If the object is materially ambiguous, ask one brief question; do not require hypotheses or a decision where the inquiry is exploratory.
- Prioritize gaps by their ability to change understanding, distinguish explanations, or affect a consequential choice. Explain the ordering without inventing numerical scores. Consider feasibility without letting easy-to-get but uninformative evidence dominate.
- For each substantive gap, explain what is missing, which judgment or distinction it bears on, why resolving it matters, and a feasible collection method or source. Describe a proposed source as a lead, not as evidence already obtained.
- Distinguish evidence not yet sought from evidence sought but unavailable. When the collection history is unknown, say so rather than inventing a search or failed request.
- For unavailable evidence, consider an accessible proxy or alternative test and explain its limits. If there is no feasible substitute, retain the unresolved uncertainty.
- Stop when the material gaps are covered. If none are apparent from the supplied context, say that rather than manufacture a wish list.

## Boundaries

- Missing evidence is not disconfirming evidence merely because it is missing. Do not declare a hypothesis false or a claim verified from a collection proposal.
- Do not repeat already supplied evidence as a gap unless its coverage or quality leaves a specific material question unresolved.
- Work directly from available context; no external tool or isolated worker is required to propose collection.
- Do not automatically execute RESEARCH, VERIFY, outreach, file writes, or other operations. INDICATORS defines developments to watch; this operation identifies evidence to seek.
